## 1. Introduction – Two-Sentence Summary

Authentication and authorization are encountered every day (login and signup screens are "authentication flows").

- **Authentication** – a mechanism to **assign an identity to a subject**; answers **"Who are you?"** in a given context (a platform, an operating system, your phone, anything).
- **Authorization** – answers **"What can you do?"** in that context: all your capabilities and permissions.

This video covers the general high-level and technical understanding: how it works, why it's important, and the components that make up authentication and authorization flows.

## 2. Historical Context of Authentication

### 2.1 Pre-industrial societies – implicit authentication

- Authentication was **intrinsic/implicit**: a person's identity was the same as their recognition in the community. Someone respected (e.g. a **village elder**) could vouch for a person. Deals were sealed with a **handshake**, symbolizing mutual recognition and agreement.
- A form of authentication based on **human contextual trust**.
- It **couldn't scale**: as populations grew and interactions went beyond familiar circles, a village elder isn't trusted across communities, countries or continents. This started the search for **explicit authentication** — proofs of identity that work independently of personal acquaintance.

### 2.2 Medieval period – seals (early cryptographic era)

- Society needed a system that scaled beyond personal recognition. The **wax seal** — a unique pattern attached to letters/agreements — acted as a signature (like today's digital or handwritten signatures).
- Seals were the **first widely adopted authentication tokens**: physical representations of identity relying on **possession — "something you have"**.
- Vulnerability: **forgery** — the first recorded instance of an **authentication bypass attack** (skipping authentication with malicious intent).
- This led to more sophisticated mechanisms (watermarks, encrypted codes in trade documentation), laying the foundation for **cryptographic thinking**.

### 2.3 Industrial Revolution – pass phrases and shared secrets

- Communication systems progressed; the **telegraph** became critical infrastructure, bringing the need for secure message validation.
- Operators used **pre-agreed pass phrases** (similar to **static passwords** — one static string) — an early form of **shared secrets**.
- The principle shifted from **"something you possess"** (wax seal) to **"something you know"** (in your brain, or communicated in writing or verbally) — the ancestor of the modern password.

### 2.4 Mainframes / digital era (mid-20th century)

- **1961 – MIT's Project MAC**, working on **CTSS (Compatible Time-Sharing System)**, introduced **passwords for multi-user systems** (letting multiple users use a computer without sharing data between each other).
- Passwords were stored in **plain text** — a critical vulnerability that came to light when someone **printed the password file**. This incident was the genesis of the **secure password storage** philosophy: passwords should not be stored in plain text.
- This led to innovations like **hashing** and cryptographic algorithms that transform passwords into **irreversible, fixed-length representations**.
    - A hashing algorithm takes a plain-text string and returns a hash string. The **length stays fixed** whether the input is 3 or 100 characters (depending on the algorithm), and the **same input always gives the same result**.
- Authentication began aligning with **confidentiality, integrity and availability**, the core tenets of information security.

### 2.5 1970s – asymmetric cryptography

- An explosion in cryptographic research, driven by **Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman**. Their **Diffie–Hellman key exchange** introduced **asymmetric cryptography**, letting two parties establish a **shared secret over an untrusted medium**.
- Asymmetric cryptography became the backbone of modern authentication protocols (also called **PKI – public key infrastructure** systems).
- This era also gave rise to **Kerberos**: **ticket-based authentication** relying on trusted third parties to issue tickets verifying both user and service identity — a precursor to today's **token-based authentication**.

### 2.6 1990s – MFA and biometrics

- The internet was growing; simple username/password systems weren't strong enough against newly emerging **brute-force and dictionary attacks**.
- **MFA (multi-factor authentication)** combines different principles:
    - **Something you know** – passwords or PINs
    - **Something you have** – cards, smart cards, OTP generators
    - **Something you are** – biometric data (fingerprints, retina scans)
- **Biometric authentication** emerged using pattern-recognition algorithms and statistical models, but introduced challenges: **false positives/negatives** and **template security**. It wasn't a one-step solution.

### 2.7 21st century

- Cloud computing, mobile devices and API-based architectures demanded advanced authentication frameworks. Important components that emerged (discussed shortly): **OAuth 2.0**, **JWTs**.
- Also mentioned: **zero trust architecture** and **passwordless authentication** such as **web authentication**, which eliminates passwords entirely, relying on **public/private keys stored in hardware devices**.

### 2.8 Candidates for the future (at the time of recording)

- **Decentralized identity** (created using technologies like blockchain) — robust features, still early stages, experimental, promising.
- **Behavioral biometrics.**
- **Post-quantum cryptography:** once quantum computers are used heavily, they'll be so fast that today's cryptographic algorithms (e.g. **RSA** and other public/private key algorithms, which are very hard to break with today's hardware) will break. Post-quantum cryptography means techniques secure even against quantum computers; some algorithms already exist.

## 3. Three Key Components: Sessions, JWTs, Cookies

### 3.1 Sessions

**Why sessions came about**

- HTTP is **stateless by design**: every request is an isolated interaction with all the information the server needs; no memory of past exchanges (see the HTTP video). This was fine for the early web of mostly static pages and read-only data, where no continuity between requests was needed.
- As the web became **dynamic** (e.g. e-commerce — remembering cart items; keeping a user logged in across pages), statelessness became a bottleneck, creating the need for **stateful interactions**.
- A **session** provides a way to establish **temporary server-side context** for each user — memory about the user for when they make their next request.

**How it works**

1. **Session creation** – when a user logs in, the server creates a **unique session ID** and stores it alongside relevant user data (role, name, cart items, whether the user is authenticated, etc.) in a **persistent store** — a database, or **Redis** (an in-memory store, as opposed to a file-based database).
2. **Session ID sent to the client as a cookie.** Every request after that includes the cookie, so the server can use the session ID to fetch the data from the store.
3. Sessions are **short-lived** (have an expiry, e.g. 15 minutes). After expiry the server creates another session, sends the new ID and stores user data again.

**Storage evolution**

- **File-based sessions** (early; data stored in files on the server) – simple, but had **scalability issues** as user numbers grew.
- **Database-backed sessions** – faster lookups and persistence across server restarts.
- **Distributed storage** – **Redis** or **Memcached** (in-memory stores; storing data in RAM rather than on hard disk), faster than DB lookups.
- Sessions are still used today for the same purpose.

### 3.2 JWTs (JSON Web Tokens)

**Why they emerged** By the mid-2000s web apps were globally distributed systems, and stateful systems caused bottlenecks:

1. **Memory/overhead** – maintaining session data for thousands/millions/billions of users is costly.
2. **Replication** – in distributed architectures, synchronizing session data across servers/regions (possibly opposite sides of the world) introduced **latency** (delays in authenticating) and **consistency challenges**.

Developers sought a solution that **offloaded state from the server** while maintaining security and integrity. **JWTs (formalized in 2015)** are a **stateless mechanism for transferring claims** between two parties/systems. The key innovation: **self-contained tokens** holding user data (user ID, role, etc.) and a cryptographic signature in one **Base64-encoded** token.

**Structure (shown on jwt.io)** – three parts:

1. **Header** – metadata about the JWT itself, such as the **signing algorithm** used.
2. **Payload** – the data. Common fields:
    - `sub` – the user ID (can be from your database, an OAuth provider, or any context; just a storage field)
    - `iat` – "issued at": when the JWT was issued
    - Optional fields: user's name, **role** (admin, member, editor, viewer, writer…), etc.
3. **Signature** – verifies you issued it and that the data hasn't been **tampered** with, using a **secret key** only you hold. If anyone changes the JWT, verification with your secret key fails and the JWT is no longer valid. This is how user data is verified **statelessly**: no session info to store; it happens on every request, is very lightweight and saves storage cost.

**Advantages**

- **Statelessness** – no server-side storage costs.
- **Scalability** – many servers use microservice/distributed architectures, where a cookie issued by one server can't be used on a server in another domain. With JWTs, the client can send the token to many servers; all share the secret key to decode and verify identity. A server can scale to many instances without worrying about user authentication, since all can authenticate users at once with a shared secret.
- **Portability** – lightweight, URL-friendly, Base64 string; can be passed between systems, stored in cookies and other limited-space storage, sent in URL values. (Can also be stored in local storage, "even though you should not do that.")

**Challenges / disadvantages**

1. **Stolen-token impersonation (name garbled in transcript)** – since JWTs are stateless, no stored mechanism validates the user's identity; if someone gets your JWT they can impersonate you and act on your behalf. There's no way to invalidate the token until it expires, unless the server **changes its secret** — which forces **all users** of the platform to log in again and get new JWTs just to invalidate one user's token.
2. **Revocation** – if the server wants to revoke a user's access (for whatever reason), it can't, because it has no way to track a JWT's status until it expires.

**Hybrid approach** (a solution to those two issues)

- Normal JWT flow: user logs in → server sends a JWT, which the user stores → on each request the user sends it (in the `Authorization` header, or another form/cookie) → the server verifies it with its secret key and identifies the user. **No additional storage lookup.**
- Hybrid: combine statelessness with statefulness. After verifying the JWT, the server also maintains a **blacklist of JWTs** in persistent storage (database, or Redis/Memcached). This lets it **temporarily block** or revoke access for some users (e.g. account hacked, malicious activity).
- **The question this raises:** the whole point of JWTs was statelessness; if you do a persistent-storage lookup anyway, why not just use the stateful approach in the first place, which is considered more secure?

**Speaker's advice**

- Don't agonize over all these strategies: **go with an auth provider** (e.g. **Auth0**, or modern alternatives like **Clerk**) — it's their headache which technologies/algorithms (hash, salt, etc.) are secure.
- If building a medium-to-large complex system, an external auth provider is a good idea.
- If you're **learning** backend engineering, it's good to **implement your own auth** to understand the parts, trade-offs, advantages and disadvantages. Then, **in a production system, go with an auth provider** unless you're very confident in your authentication workflows.

### 3.3 Cookies

- A cookie is a way of **storing a piece of information (any string/value) in the user's browser from the server side** — a nice feature offered by browsers; via the HTTP request workflow, servers can store info in the browser.
- A cookie is **only accessible to the server that set it**; a server can't see other servers' cookies (a nice browser security feature).
- A cookie set by a server is **sent with all subsequent requests to that server**, enabling the authentication workflow:
    1. The client authenticates with username/password.
    2. If successful, the server **sets a cookie** containing the authorization token — a JWT, a session ID, or whatever the implementation uses.
    3. In subsequent requests the browser (Chrome, Firefox, etc.) sends the cookie to the server, so the server can validate the token, identify the user and authorize them.
- Cookies automate the process of the server sending the token to the client and the client sending it back with every request, all driven from the server side.

## 4. Types of Authentication

Four major types: **stateful**, **stateless**, **API key-based**, and **OAuth 2.0 / OpenID Connect**.

### 4.1 Stateful Authentication

**Flow**

1. The client (browser) sends username (or email) and password to the server.
2. The server checks validity/correctness; if the user is eligible, it **generates a session ID**, bundles it with the user's data and **stores it in Redis** (a database could also be used, but most platforms use Redis for its fast read/access time).
3. The server sends the **session ID back in a cookie** — an **HTTP-only cookie**, meaning **JavaScript can't access** the session ID value.
4. Due to how cookies attach to subsequent requests, all following requests carry the cookie. The server takes the session ID, checks its existence and expiry in Redis plus the user data, identifies/authorizes the user, and the user can call the API.

- The session ID can be any cryptographically random string, or even a JWT, depending on the implementation.

### 4.2 Stateless Authentication

**Flow**

1. The client sends email and password in the login request.
2. If correct, the server generates a **signed JWT** using its **secret key** (which must be stored securely so it can sign and verify JWTs). The JWT contains user info like user ID and role.
3. The server sends the JWT to the client.
4. In subsequent requests, the client sends the JWT — the standard way being a header called **`Authorization`**.
5. The server extracts the token and **verifies it with the secret key**, finds the user's ID, and on success the user gets a successful API response; otherwise the server throws an error such as **unauthorized or forbidden** (per convention).

- Called **stateless** because it needs no lookup in a persistent store; everything needed about the user is in the signed, self-contained JWT, and the secret key verifies its authenticity.

### 4.3 Stateful vs Stateless – Pros and Cons

**Stateful pros**

- **Centralized control** over all sessions; **real-time information** about active sessions (via the persistent store).
- Easy to **revoke access**, revoke a session or log a user out.
- Well-suited to applications with modest traffic and strict session requirements.
- Speaker's opinion: **most applications should go with stateful authentication**, because of its secure nature and the convenience of revoking tokens/access and having real-time information about users' access.

**Stateful challenges**

- Limited scalability; higher operational complexity with distributed systems (multiple servers in multiple regions → latency issues).

**Stateless pros** (the cons of stateful)

- Scalability, no session-store dependency, ideal for **distributed architectures** and **mobile-friendly** applications where cookies aren't a thing.

**Stateless cons**

- **Token revocation is complex.** Once a JWT is issued, you can't revoke the user's access until it expires — a problem if an account is compromised and the user contacts support asking to revoke the session. The only option is changing the JWT secret key, which logs out **all** users.

**Hybrid suggestion**

- Use **stateful** authentication for the **main web app running in a browser**, and **stateless** authentication for clients using your APIs from **mobile apps or third-party/server integrations**, for scalability and simplicity.

### 4.4 API Key-Based Authentication

Serves a completely different set of use cases from stateful/stateless.

**How it works**

- On a platform's UI you click **generate an API key** and get a **cryptographically random string**, which you use to gain access to the server behind that UI.
- **Example (ChatGPT / OpenAI):** ChatGPT's UI is what users interact with, but servers in the cloud produce the responses. OpenAI also offers **API keys** for people who want **programmatic access** — e.g. to build their own UI using the model's responses, or to get the model's responses programmatically inside their own server (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, etc.), without using ChatGPT's UI.
- API keys provide access to the **server, not the UI/platform**, to a different set of users in a **confined, permission-based, expiry-based** manner. The key (with a set of permissions and expiry dates) is attached in headers (or as the server's implementation requires) and lets you do through the API what the platform can do.

**Advantages**

1. **Easy to generate** – go to the UI, click generate, and use the key to access the server programmatically.
2. **Ideal for machine-to-machine communication.**
    - Client-to-server communication: a UI, human interaction with mouse/keyboard; the UI sends programmatic requests; the server responds; the user sees the rendered result. Login flows (typing username/password, server validation, receiving and saving a JWT) require human triggers.
    - **Machine-to-machine:** e.g. my server (with its own UI for users) needs ChatGPT's capabilities to summarize a paragraph the user typed. My server uses the API key generated on the ChatGPT platform to request the ChatGPT server, which identifies/authorizes me (plan, remaining quota, access, etc., all maintained on their side via the key). My server gets the response and sends it back to my UI. There's no UI or human interaction in the server-to-server step.
    - The simple workflow: you give the machine a **secret key**, it saves it in an **environment variable or secure storage**, and sends it with every request to identify itself — instead of username/password/token workflows.

### 4.5 OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect

**Why it was needed** Previous approaches are a simple setup: create an account with username/password and authenticate each time. The more sites you visit, the more credentials you create and maintain. Problems:

1. **Security risk** – reusing passwords (e.g. `12345` or `password`) was very common in the early internet era; a single breach compromised many accounts. (Today people are more aware and use password generators/managers.)
2. **Fatigue** – too many accounts to manage.

**Delegation** (a very important OAuth concept)

- Websites started needing access to **other websites' resources**. Examples: a travel app (hotel/train booking) needing your Gmail to programmatically scan flight tickets; a social media app wanting to import contacts from another platform or from Google contacts. One platform wants to import resources from another programmatically.
- **Initial solution: sharing passwords** — disastrous:
    - Gives **full access** to everything in your account; no way to limit permissions.
    - **Impossible to revoke access** without changing your password everywhere (since passwords were reused across platforms and one platform now holds your password).

**Birth of OAuth (2007) – "Open Authorization"**

- A revolutionary idea, not just a protocol. Engineers from companies like Google and Twitter worked on standardizing a way for users to **share access between platforms** (the delegation problem) **without sharing passwords**.
- Solution: **share tokens, not passwords.** A password gives all access to your account; a **token has specific permissions to a specific part** of your account.
    - Example: sharing a Google password gives access to photos, Maps history, contacts, calendar, with the ability to add/remove/delete everything. A token that can only **read contacts** (not delete/add) gives permission for a specific part of a specific platform.

**Four components**

1. **Resource owner** – the user who owns the data (you).
2. **Client** – the app requesting access (e.g. Facebook requesting your contacts from your Google account).
3. **Resource server** – holds the resource (your Google account/Google's server).
4. **Authorization server** – issues the token after authenticating the user.

**OAuth 1.0 flow**

1. The client (Facebook) **redirects the user to the authorization server**.
2. The user **authenticates and grants permission** ("yes, I allow" these permissions).
3. The authorization server **sends a token to the client**.
4. The client uses the token to **access the resources** (your contacts on the Google server) programmatically, with specific permissions, without you sharing your password.

**OAuth 2.0 (around 2010)** – OAuth 1.0 was revolutionary but had limitations:

- **Complex for developers** to implement; the whole flow was complicated.
- It used **cryptographic signatures**, which were **error-prone**.

OAuth 2.0 changes:

- Introduced **bearer tokens** — much simpler (though more vulnerable), making implementations simpler.
- Lets developers **choose flows based on app type** (mobile, server-side, device-only like a smart TV). Common flows:
    1. **Authorization code flow** – for **server-side apps**.
    2. **Implicit flow** – for browser-based apps; **now discouraged due to security risks**.
    3. **Client credentials flow** – for **machine-to-machine** communication (e.g. between two servers) without any user, browser or human interaction.
    4. **Device code flow** – for devices with **limited input** (no keyboard/mouse), e.g. **smart TV** authentication.

**OpenID Connect (OIDC, ~2014)**

- OAuth is great for **authorization** (via delegation) but **didn't solve authentication** (who you are: identity, name, ID), versus authorization (what you can do).
- **OIDC is built on top of OAuth 2.0's security mechanisms** to fill the authentication gap. It introduced the **ID token** — typically a **JWT** — containing information such as the user's ID, when they logged in (`iat`), the **issuing authority** (who issued the token), and the user's name, email, etc. that the relying party wants.
- This is what powers **"Sign in with Google / Facebook / Discord"**: your identity (email, profile picture, name, whatever profile info is requested) is taken from Google and used to identify/authenticate you without the platform having its own authentication mechanisms.

**OIDC flow (example: a note-taking app with "Sign in with Google")**

1. The client (note-taking app) **redirects the user to the authorization server** (Google's).
2. The user logs in with their Google account **on Google's authorization server** (not the client's server) and grants the requested permissions (e.g. reading email, name, profile picture, or Google Keep access).
3. The authorization server sends the client an **authorization code** and an **ID token** (the ID token is optional at this stage in some workflows).
4. The client **exchanges the authorization code with the resource server for an access token** on behalf of the user — and an **ID token**, if it didn't get one in the first step (it's a JWT with the user's ID, name, and other values).
5. Using the **access token**, the client can do operations on the user's behalf in the resource server — e.g. fetch all notes from Google Keep — **only** with the permissions granted.

**Summary:** OAuth 2.0 and OIDC are like "security guards or key makers" of the digital age: no user or platform gets more access than needed or than they asked for. Together they transformed the internet from "password-sharing chaos" into a secure, modern, interconnected system, enabling resource and permission sharing across platforms.

### 4.6 When to Use Which (rules of thumb)

|Type|Ideal for|
|---|---|
|**Stateful** (session ID or JWT + persistent storage)|Typical **web app** authentication workflows — a large portion of SaaS-based models; user-specific session data stored on the server|
|**Stateless**|**APIs** or **scalable systems** with distributed servers around the world, where tokens carry user data|
|**OAuth**|**Third-party integrations** and **login via external providers** (Google, Facebook, Discord)|
|**API key**|**Server-to-server / machine-to-machine** communication, or single-purpose client access to APIs|

- In the speaker's experience, you'll use **stateful and stateless authentication most of the time** when building APIs.

## 5. Authorization

- Recap: **authentication = who** the user is (identity); **authorization = what** the user can do (permissions).

### Why authorization was needed (note-taking platform example)

- A user logs in with their credentials and can create, update and delete notes.
- Your platform's **delete** doesn't permanently delete: notes go to a **trash/recycle bin**, and after **30 days** they move to another zone (the speaker's "dead zone") — deleted for the user but **not permanently deleted from the database**.
- As creator/administrator you want to access these notes programmatically through an **admin UI**, with capabilities **not granted to every user**.
- **Naive solution:** send a random "god-mode" string with each API request so the server identifies the creator. **Problems:**
    - **Security flaw:** if someone intercepts the string they could do disastrous things — clean the DB, mess with other users' data.
    - **Scaling access:** to give the same special access to other users (friends, maintainers), you'd have to share the string or create more strings and add server support for them. The system gets more complicated, hard to manage and more prone to security flaws.
- **Authorization** = providing specific permissions to specific users on a platform. Not all users have the same level of access: some have more capabilities, some fewer, some a completely different set.
- In **multi-tenant architectures** (users create organizations), the **admin** needs to assign different permissions to members — e.g. read/write to some, read-only to others.

### RBAC – Role-Based Access Control

- One of the most famous authorization techniques a backend engineer should know.
- A platform has **roles** (e.g. **user**, **admin**, **moderator**), and each role is assigned a set of **permissions**.
    - Example: user role → read only; admin role → read and write; moderator role → read and write (as given in the transcript). You can create **custom roles** with their own permissions on different resources.
    - Example: users get read/write/delete on notes, but access to the "dead zone" notes is provided to **admin only**. You can be as granular as you want.

**Typical workflow**

1. A user registers/signs up and the server **assigns a role** (user, or admin depending on the use case).
2. In subsequent requests, the user sends its identification — a **session ID** (stateful) or a **JWT** (stateless).
3. The server **deduces the user's role** from the token, or via a **database lookup**.
4. The role is determined **early in the request cycle** and passed to the next set of **middlewares** (covered in a future video): the first entry point checks identity and role and attaches that information so later logic can decide whether the user can access, e.g., dead-zone notes.
5. If the role is `user`, the API fails and the server returns **403 Forbidden** — you don't have enough permission to perform this task or access this resource.

## 6. Two Security Points for Backend Engineers

### 6.1 Error Messages in Authentication

- Specific messages are helpful to legitimate users but give attackers clues:
    - "**User not found**" → the attacker learns the username doesn't exist and moves on to the next user.
    - "**Incorrect password**" → the attacker now knows the username is correct and only needs to attack the password (brute force, dictionary attacks), increasing their attack surface.
    - "**Account locked due to too many failed attempts**" → also gives information.
- **Rule:** never send specific or user-friendly messages in the authentication workflow. Always send a **generic message** (e.g. **"authentication failed"**) for all failure cases, so an attacker is left confused about the next step.
- User-friendly messages are fine in other workflows (e.g. validations or other APIs).

### 6.2 Timing Attacks

A rare kind of attack. Typical authentication workflow:

1. **Find the user** (by email/username).
2. **Check if the account is locked/suspended** (servers lock accounts for some time — e.g. 2 minutes, 4 minutes, a day — after too many invalid attempts).
3. **Compare the password**: passwords aren't stored in plain text; they're hashed into a cryptographically safe string. The server hashes the provided password with the same algorithm/key and compares it to the stored hash. If they differ, the password is incorrect.

**The problem:** if the username is invalid, the system stops at step 1 and responds **faster**. If the username is valid but the password is wrong, it fails at step 3 and the response is **a little slower**, since hashing takes longer than the other operations. Attackers can use this delay (e.g. ~200 ms) to tell whether the username was valid or the password was wrong, then decide whether to target the username or the password via brute force/dictionary attacks.

**Defenses** — equalize response times:

1. Use **constant-time operations** — cryptographically secure **constant-time comparison functions** for password hashes, so execution time doesn't vary based on input similarity.
2. **Simulate a response delay** using your language's facilities (e.g. `setTimeout` in Node, `time.Sleep` in Go) — e.g. even if the username doesn't match, don't respond immediately; add a fake delay of ~200 ms so attackers can't measure timing differences between username failures and password failures.
