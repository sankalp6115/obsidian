Your food order is stuck. The payment service is not responding. So, what should our app do? Normally, everything works. A user sends a request to the food delivery app. The app calls the payment service, and the payment succeeds. Suddenly, the payment service goes down. Requests start failing. But, the app may keep trying. Retry after retry after retry. And now, we have a bigger problem. One failure creates more requests. More requests make the failing service even harder to recover. The system is wasting resources. We need a way to stop calling a service that is already failing. This is where the circuit breaker helps. Think of it like an electrical circuit breaker. When something goes badly wrong, it cuts the connection. Closed means everything is normal. Requests are allowed through to the payment service. Now, the payment service starts failing. The breaker watches those failures and keeps track of them. If there are too many failures, the circuit opens. New requests are stopped. We fail fast instead of waiting for the broken service. This is the important part. The request reaches the circuit breaker and is rejected immediately. The app does not wait for the broken service. But, should the breaker stay open forever? No. After waiting for some time, the breaker enters the half-open state and allows a small test request. The test request succeeds. That tells us the payment service has recovered. So, we close the circuit and allow normal traffic again. But, what if the test fails? The service is still unhealthy. So, the breaker opens again. The circuit breaker has three states. Closed means requests flow. Open means requests stop. Half-open means we test recovery. If the test succeeds, we go back to closed. Without a circuit breaker, requests keep waiting, retries pile up, resources get exhausted, and the whole application can become slow. The circuit breaker protects the rest of the system. Circuit breaker means this. When a dependency keeps failing, stop sending requests for a while. Give it time to recover, then send a small test. If it works, close the circuit. If it fails, open it again.
